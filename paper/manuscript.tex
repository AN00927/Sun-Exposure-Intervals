\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Public-health guidance on sun exposure implicitly assumes a trade-off:
more ultraviolet (UV) means more vitamin D and also more skin-cancer
risk, so advice must sit somewhere between them. We show, in a
three-state mechanistic model of serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D
(25(OH)D) dynamics under photoadaptation, that for a \emph{fixed}
total UV budget this trade-off does not exist: the schedule that
maximises 25(OH)D is also the schedule that minimises modelled
melanoma and basal-cell-carcinoma risk. Both point to frequent, small,
sub-erythemal sessions.

We separate two distinct claims that the phrase ``front-loading''
conflates. Concentrating a fixed \emph{weekly} budget into fewer days
lowers steady-state 25(OH)D monotonically, by 9.53--28.59\,\% across
nine skin-type $\times$ budget cells (absolute-SED weekly budgets of
1.75, 3.5 and 7.0, i.e.\ 0.25--1.0\,SED/day averaged over the week),
and does so in 9 of 9 cells; the mechanism (dose-response concavity,
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}) is protected by the available per-session
curvature evidence at the two higher budgets but not at the lowest
(0.25\,SED/day), where the evidence neither supports nor contradicts
it. Redistributing a fixed \emph{horizon} budget from a flat to a
front-loaded profile, at matched session count, does not move the
plateau at all (mean $+0.38\,\%$ over 84 days; $-7.40\,\%$ over 365
days). What front-loading buys is transient, not terminal: it reduces
accumulated deficiency below the sufficiency threshold by a mean of
47.1\,\% at 84 days and 63.8\,\% at 365 days, and reaches sufficiency
in a median of 4 days rather than 37.

Mechanism ablation attributes the ordering to the concavity of the
logarithmic dose-response term alone; disabling product-feedback
inhibition or the photoadaptation channels changes the magnitude of
the gap but never its sign. Under a risk-constrained optimisation, all
six Fitzpatrick skin types select the shortest available interval.

We state the limits rather than minimise them. Four parameter pairs
are jointly unidentifiable; the single genuinely held-out external
benchmark \emph{fails, narrowly}, running just above a band drawn
from one source study; and the synthesis scalar is calibrated on
Fitzpatrick I--II subjects only, making every absolute prediction for
darker skin an explicit extrapolation. The interval ranking survives
all of this because it depends on the shape of the dose-response
rather than its scale. No absolute duration reported here should be
read as a dosing instruction.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Author summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Author summary}

Advice about sunlight usually assumes you must choose between making
enough vitamin D and keeping your skin-cancer risk low. Using a
computational model of how vitamin D builds up and decays in the body,
we find that for a fixed amount of total sun, you do not have to
choose. Many short exposures beat a few long ones on both counts at
once.

The reason is a mathematical property rather than a biological one.
Vitamin D synthesis rises with the logarithm of UV dose, so it
saturates: the second half of a long session yields less than the
first half did. Splitting the same total dose into more, smaller
sessions therefore produces more vitamin D, at least at the moderate
daily doses (roughly half a standard erythemal dose per day or more)
this paper's own evidence review can vouch for; at very low daily
doses the published data neither confirm nor rule out the same effect.
Independently, the epidemiology of melanoma tracks intermittent bursts
of intense exposure rather than accumulated dose, so the same split
schedule is also the lower-risk one.

We also separate two things people mean by ``front-loading''. Getting
your weekly sun in fewer days is worse. Getting more of your sun early
in a longer programme does not raise where you eventually end up, but
it does get you there much faster, and time spent deficient is itself
a harm worth avoiding.

We are explicit about what the model cannot support. It reproduces the
ordering of schedules reliably; it does not reliably predict how many
minutes any particular person needs.

\section{Introduction}

Cutaneous synthesis is the dominant source of vitamin D for most
populations, and the dose-response linking ultraviolet exposure to a
rise in serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) is well characterised in
its broad shape: the response is positive, saturating, and modulated
by constitutive pigmentation \citep{webb2021,young2021pnas,young2020}.
What remains contested is not whether sunlight raises 25(OH)D but how
exposure should be \emph{arranged in time}. Guidance is typically
issued as a duration and a frequency --- some minutes, some number of
times per week --- without an explicit account of why that particular
partition of total exposure was chosen over another delivering the
same cumulative dose.

The question has a natural adversarial framing. Photoadaptation
supplies a mechanism by which frequent exposure should become
progressively less productive: repeated UV drives facultative melanin
upregulation and stratum corneum thickening, both of which attenuate
subsequent synthesis \citep{deboer2025,pearse1987,datta2019}.
Product-feedback inhibition supplies a second: as 25(OH)D rises,
further synthesis is inhibited, so a subject held near sufficiency
converts each new dose less efficiently. If either effect is strong
enough, spacing exposures further apart --- allowing pigment to fade
and serum levels to fall between sessions --- could plausibly extract
more 25(OH)D per unit of UV than daily exposure does. That hypothesis
is the direct motivation for the present analysis.

Set against it is a purely mathematical consideration. If synthesis
per session is concave in dose, then for any fixed total budget,
subdividing that budget into more and smaller sessions must increase
total synthesis, by Jensen's inequality, irrespective of any state
dynamics. Which of these two forces dominates is an empirical question
about parameter magnitudes, and it is not answerable by inspection.

A second question runs alongside the first. The scheduling literature
generally treats vitamin D benefit and skin-cancer risk as opposed,
because both increase with UV. But the epidemiology does not support
treating ``UV'' as one exposure. Squamous cell carcinoma tracks
\emph{cumulative} dose, whereas melanoma and basal cell carcinoma
track \emph{intermittent}, intense exposure --- and heavy continuous
occupational exposure is associated with a \emph{lower} melanoma odds
ratio than intermittent recreational exposure
\citep{elwood1997,leiter2009,calzavarapinton2015}. If risk is scored
on the pathway that actually carries it, the direction of the
supposed trade-off is no longer obvious.

This paper addresses three questions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item At a fixed UV budget, does concentrating exposure into fewer
      sessions raise or lower steady-state 25(OH)D, and by how much?
\item Which mechanism produces that ordering --- dose-response
      concavity, photoadaptation, or feedback inhibition?
\item Do the vitamin D objective and the skin-cancer objective select
      the same schedule or different ones?
\end{enumerate}

We answer them within a single frozen exposure scenario, with the
model's uncertainty, its unidentifiable parameter combinations, and
its one out-of-sample test reported alongside the results rather than
after them. That reporting posture is deliberate. An
earlier iteration of this work published a headline optimum derived
from an objective function that had no interior optimum; the
safeguards described in Section~\ref{sec:objectives} exist because of
that failure.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Model structure}

We model three coupled states: effective serum 25(OH)D, $D(t)$
(\nmol); an effective melanin index, $M(t)$, dimensionless on the
Datta reflectance scale where $0$ denotes Fitzpatrick skin type (FST)
I and $1$ denotes FST VI \citep{datta2019}; and additional stratum
corneum thickness above baseline, $\mathrm{SC}(t)$, dimensionless.

\begin{align}
\frac{dD}{dt}  &= S\big(UV(t), M, \mathrm{SC}, D, F, b\big) - k_{\mathrm{decay}}(D)\, D \\[2pt]
\frac{dM}{dt}  &= \alpha\, UV(t)\,\big(M_{\max} - M\big) - \beta\big(M - M_{\mathrm{base}}(F)\big) \\[2pt]
\frac{d\mathrm{SC}}{dt} &= \alpha_{\mathrm{SC}}\, UV(t)\,\big(\mathrm{SC}_{\max} - \mathrm{SC}\big) - k_{\mathrm{decay,SC}}\,\mathrm{SC} - k_{\mathrm{damage}}\, UV(t)\, \mathrm{SC}
\end{align}

The synthesis term takes the logarithmic form reported by Webb and
colleagues \citep{webb2021}, attenuated multiplicatively by
constitutive pigment, facultative pigment, and stratum corneum, scaled
by exposed body surface area, and inhibited by accumulated product:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:synth}
S = \underbrace{A \ln\!\big(1 + UV \cdot E_{\mathrm{source}}\big)}_{\text{concave in dose}}
\cdot\, \phi(b)
\cdot\, e^{-K_{\mathrm{const}} M_{\mathrm{base}}(F)}
\cdot\, e^{-K_{\mathrm{ATTEN}} \max(0,\, M - M_{\mathrm{base}}(F))}
\cdot\, e^{-K_{\mathrm{SC}} \mathrm{SC}}
\cdot\, g(D)
\end{equation}

where $\phi(b) = (b/b_{\mathrm{ref}})^{0.5}$ is a sub-linear
body-surface-area factor and $g(D)$ is the feedback factor. The
feedback form in force throughout this paper is the biphasic
$g(D) = 1/\big(1 + (D/56.0)^2\big)$, whose inflection is an output of
a breakpoint fitted on the vitamin D$_3$ axis \citep{scholze2026}
rather than a free regressor. Decay is state-dependent, following a
power law fitted to the three-group means of Datta and colleagues
\citep{datta2017}; the fit reproduces all three reported half-lives
to within 1.4\,\%.

Equation~\eqref{eq:synth} is the analytical heart of this paper. The
first factor is concave, and no other factor depends on how a fixed
budget is partitioned within a day. The remaining factors are all
state-dependent attenuations bounded in $(0,1]$. Whether concavity or
attenuation dominates the schedule ordering is settled empirically in
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}.

Parameter values and their full source derivations are not restated
here. The module docstring of \texttt{vitd\_model.py} is the single
canonical source in the accompanying repository, and it records for
each parameter the study, the arithmetic, and the evidence status.

\subsection{Frozen reference scenario}

Every schedule in this paper is evaluated under one fixed exposure
scenario, held identical on every exposure day: a constant ambient UV
index, a fixed horizontal-to-personal geometry factor
\citep{cockell2001}, and a fixed exposed body surface area of 35\,\%,
corresponding to a t-shirt-and-shorts configuration with partial
coverage. The constants are defined in \texttt{vitd\_scenario.py},
which is their only canonical source.

The scenario is a deliberate scope restriction. An audit of an earlier
version of this work found that a location-based dosimetry chain
--- solar position to ambient UV index to posture and site geometry to
exposed body surface area --- stacked four independent and separately
plausible uncertainties into a single product that had never been
validated as a product. That chain was retired. Nothing it produces
reaches any number reported here.

The consequence must be stated plainly: every duration in this paper
is a duration \emph{under these reference conditions}, not a calendar
recommendation for a place or a date. Real ambient UV varies with
latitude, season, time of day, altitude, cloud and ozone; real
personal dose varies with posture and clothing. We do not model that
variation.

\subsection{Objective hierarchy and its safeguards}
\label{sec:objectives}

The model admits several scoring functions, and they do not agree. To
prevent the recurrence of a specific past failure --- reporting as
advice an optimum drawn from a metric with no interior optimum --- the
objectives are ranked in code, and report-generating routines that
attempt to present a diagnostic-tier metric as a recommendation raise
an exception rather than print.

\begin{description}
\item[Primary.] Minimum dose and fewest sessions to reach and then
      hold sufficiency, subject to a sub-erythemal per-session cap and
      a practical session-length ceiling. Everything reported as
      advice derives from here.
\item[Secondary.] The tri-objective frontier and the fixed-budget risk
      minimisation. These refine a recommendation; they never replace
      one.
\item[Diagnostic.] Per-\SED{} efficiency ratios. Informative about the
      model, barred from being reported as advice.
\end{description}

The demotion of the per-\SED{} efficiency metric is re-derived on
every run rather than remembered. Its argmax is recomputed under three
different search bounds --- a genuine peak does not move when the
bound moves, and this one does --- and the terminal 25(OH)D at that
argmax is compared against baseline. It selects schedules that finish
\emph{below} their starting concentration: a net loss of vitamin D
scored as maximal efficiency.

\subsection{Experimental arms}

We distinguish two operations that the term ``front-loading'' conflates,
and analyse them separately.

\paragraph{Arm (a): within-week concentration.} The weekly \SED{}
budget is held fixed and only the number of days over which it is
spread varies, from daily down to a single weekly session. The pattern
repeats weekly, so nothing is front-loaded across the horizon. This is
the question in its literal form.

\paragraph{Arm (b): across-horizon taper.} Total \SED{} over the whole
horizon is held fixed and the dose \emph{profile} is varied from flat
to front-loaded-and-tapering (two-phase and exponential families),
crossed with a one-parameter monotone spacing warp in which
$\gamma = 1$ is uniform spacing and $\gamma > 1$ is front-loaded.
Every profile is rescaled to deliver exactly the same total \SED{}, so
only shape is swept.

Arm (b) is referenced throughout to the flat, uniformly spaced arm at
the \emph{same session count}. This matching is not cosmetic. An
unmatched comparison, which lets the tapered arm also use a different
number of sessions, shows a gain of several percent that is an
artefact of the session-count difference rather than of dose shape.

Both arms use a deterministic grid sweep with \emph{no} constraint
applied, deliberately rather than the constrained optimisers described
below, because a sufficiency floor and an erythema cap would silently
delete exactly the concentrated arms the question is about. Since no
cap is applied, every row instead carries its largest single session
as a fraction of the minimal erythemal dose (MED), checked against a
sub-erythemal ceiling of 70\,\% MED and against frank burn at
1.0 MED. An arm that wins while requiring a burn has not won, and is
flagged as such.

\subsection{Mechanism ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}

Schedule comparisons are run at three ablation rungs: melanin dynamics
only; melanin plus stratum corneum; and melanin plus stratum corneum
with skin-cancer indices reported. The third rung is
\emph{dynamically identical} to the second by construction --- it is a
reporting obligation, requiring the analysis to display the risk
indices the simulator computes regardless, not an addition of cancer
risk to the dynamics.

A caveat governs how the first rung may be read. Model parameters were
fitted with the stratum corneum channel present, and nothing is
re-fitted when it is removed. Deleting an attenuating channel without
re-estimating the terms it is collinear with inflates predicted
synthesis by an uncharacterised amount. Absolute 25(OH)D values under
that rung are therefore \emph{not} calibrated predictions and are not
comparable across rungs; only \emph{within}-rung schedule rankings are
defensible. Every ranking we report from that rung is accompanied by
the same test repeated at all three rungs, so that the reader can see
whether the sign survives.

A separate, self-contained sub-study additionally ablates each
mechanism individually: photoadaptation off, stratum corneum off,
melanin adaptation off, feedback $g(D)$ off, and decay off, together
with a sweep of the facultative-melanin attenuation coefficient across
two orders of magnitude beyond its calibrated value.

\subsection{Skin-cancer risk model}

Risk is scored on two epidemiologically distinct axes, reported
separately, with a geometric mean retained only as a ranking
convenience that is not itself literature-anchored. The cumulative
axis is a power law rather than a linear term: incidence follows
$\mathrm{dose}^{A_b}$ with a biological amplification factor of
$1.6$--$1.8$ for squamous cell carcinoma, so doubling cumulative dose
costs a factor of $3.25$ rather than $2$. The intermittent axis rises
steeply with per-session dose, following the intermittent-exposure
odds ratios reported in the epidemiological literature
\citep{elwood1997,leiter2009,calzavarapinton2015}.

The skin-type axis is not a prior. It is the modelled fraction of DNA
damage reaching the germinative basal layer, derived from measured
melanin percentages and basal-to-whole cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer
protection ratios \citep{shih2018,fajuyigbe2018}. It reproduces a
published melanoma relative risk for FST I versus FST IV (2.09
measured against 2.12 derived) without having been fitted to it. That
external agreement is the strongest single piece of corroboration in
the risk model, and it is limited: the underlying protection-ratio
measurement covers only extreme phototypes, so FST III, IV and V rest
on the melanin percentages alone.

\subsection{Constrained optimisation}

Where a single recommendation is required rather than a frontier, we
minimise the combined risk index subject to: sufficiency
($\ge 50$~\nmol) on every day after a ramp-in period; a per-session
dose at or below 70\,\% MED; and a session length within a practical
ceiling. Spacing and dose are searched jointly using a dose vector in
which a zero entry encodes ``no session that day'', so timing and dose
are represented without integer variables. The comparison arm pins
spacing to uniform and is searched by the same solver at the same
budget, because population size scales with parameter count and an
extra free dimension would otherwise confound spacing with search
effort.

\subsection{Sensitivity and identifiability}

We report one-at-a-time sensitivity over documented prior ranges,
pairwise identifiability computed as the fraction of the
steady-state-D response surface explained by a single linear
combination, and Monte Carlo uncertainty propagation over 400 draws
uniform on those ranges.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Concentrating a fixed weekly budget is monotonically worse}

Holding the weekly \SED{} budget fixed and varying only the number of
days across which it is delivered, steady-state 25(OH)D falls
monotonically as exposure is concentrated. Moving from a session every
day to a single weekly session changes steady-state 25(OH)D by
between $-9.53\,\%$ and $-28.59\,\%$ across the nine skin-type
$\times$ budget cells, and steady-state 25(OH)D rises monotonically
with sessions per week in 9 of those 9 cells.

The effect is largest at the largest budgets, which is the signature
of a saturating dose-response rather than of an adaptive state: at
\SI{7}{\SED\per week} the penalty for weekly concentration reaches
$-28.59\,\%$ at FST V and $-26.56\,\%$ at FST VI, whereas at
\SI{1.75}{\SED\per week} it is $-11.85\,\%$ and $-9.53\,\%$
respectively. A larger session sits further out on the concave part of
the curve, so more of it is wasted.

Concentration is also unsafe at its extreme. The worst arm on the grid
--- FST II, \SI{7}{\SED\per week} delivered in one session --- reaches
269\,\% of one MED in a single exposure, a frank burn. The
schedules that lose on vitamin D are, at this end of the grid, the
same schedules that are dangerous.

The deficiency consequences are larger than the plateau differences
suggest, though the recalibration has changed which schedules clear
the sufficiency floor at all. At FST V and \SI{7}{\SED\per week},
splitting across seven days now reaches sufficiency on day 48 and
accumulates \SI{515.2}{\nmol\per\litre\day} of deficit; the same
budget in one weekly session never reaches sufficiency and
accumulates \SI{1249.4}{}. At FST VI, \emph{neither} arrangement of
this budget now reaches sufficiency within the horizon: the split
schedule accumulates \SI{912.0}{\nmol\per\litre\day} of deficit
against \SI{1589.2}{} for the one-session-a-week arrangement. This is
a qualitative change from the pre-recalibration model, in which the
split FST VI schedule reached sufficiency on day 51; under the
current parameters it no longer does, at this budget.

\subsection{Redistributing a fixed horizon budget does not move the plateau}

Arm (b) gives a null on the terminal measure. Over 36 matched-N cells
(2052 front-loaded arms), the best front-loaded profile differs from
flat-and-uniform on steady-state 25(OH)D by a mean of $+0.38\,\%$ at
an 84-day horizon, with the largest positive plateau gain available
anywhere on the front-loaded half of the grid being $+0.96\,\%$.

Extending the horizon does not give the shape families room to work;
it removes what little effect there was. At 365 days the same
comparison yields a mean of $-7.40\,\%$, and the largest positive
plateau gain available anywhere on the front-loaded half of the grid
is $+0.00\,\%$. This forecloses the obvious objection that the
three-parameter shape families were simply horizon-limited.

The steady-state level is therefore set by the budget and by how
finely that budget is divided across days --- arm (a) --- and not by
how the same budget is arranged over the horizon.

\subsection{Front-loading buys the transient, not the plateau}

Ranked instead on accumulated deficiency below the sufficiency
threshold, a horizon-integrated rather than terminal measure, the
picture inverts from a null to a large effect.

At the 84-day horizon, front-loading produces a mean 47.1\,\%
reduction in accumulated deficiency relative to flat-and-uniform at
the same session count (maximum 98.9\,\%), or 41.5\,\% when the search
is restricted to sub-erythemal arms only. The deficit-optimal spacing
exponent is $\gamma = 2.5$, and it reaches sufficiency in a median of
4 days against 37 days at the spacing that maximises the terminal
plateau instead.

At 365 days the reduction is a mean of 63.8\,\% (maximum 99.9\,\%),
59.2\,\% among sub-erythemal arms, reaching sufficiency in a median of
7 days against 231 days for the plateau-maximising spacing. The
deficit-optimal spacing exponent at this horizon is no longer a
single value but a tie among $\gamma \in \{1.4, 1.8, 2.5\}$.

The effect survives the erythema cap, so it is not purchased with a
burn.

\subsection{The two measures conflict, and the conflict widens with horizon}

No taper sign may be stated without naming the measure it was read
from. Across the spacing exponent at 84 days, the terminal measure
moves by approximately 6 percentage points while the horizon-integrated
measure moves by approximately 107; the terminal argmax lies at
$\gamma \in \{0.8, 1, 1.4, 1.8, 2.5\}$ and the integrated argmax at
$\gamma = 2.5$. At this horizon the two measures are not yet pulled
apart into an artefact: the terminal winner ($\gamma = 1.4$) beats the
flat reference by only $+1.0\,\%$ and carries $0.92\times$ its
accumulated deficiency, i.e.\ slightly \emph{less}, not more. At 365
days the terminal measure moves by approximately 38 points against
approximately 355 for the integrated one, and the argmaxes separate
completely: $\gamma = 0.4$ against $\gamma \in \{1.4, 1.8, 2.5\}$.

The terminal winner at the long horizon is an artefact and must not be
quoted bare. Steady-state 25(OH)D is computed as a mean over the final
28 days, so at a 365-day horizon the averaging window is under 8\,\%
of the run and any schedule that piles dose into the endpoint wins it.
The arm that maximises the terminal plateau does so by
\emph{back}-loading at $\gamma = 0.4$: it opens with a 35-day
no-exposure gap, stacks dose beneath the averaging window, wins the
terminal measure by $+22.2\,\%$, and pays for it with $11.1\times$
worse accumulated deficiency than the flat reference. It leaves the
subject deficient for most of the year.

A related caution applies to corroboration. Steady-state 25(OH)D and
final 25(OH)D are \emph{both} terminal measures, and as the horizon
grows the averaging window shrinks toward the endpoint, so the two
converge. Their agreement at long horizons is co-movement between two
views of the same instant, not independent confirmation, and must
never be presented as two metrics agreeing.

\subsection{The ordering comes from concavity, not from adaptation}
\label{sec:mechanism}

The interval ordering is produced by neither photoadaptation nor
feedback inhibition. Disabling $g(D)$ entirely leaves the ordering
unchanged and \emph{widens} daily dosing's margin rather than
narrowing it; disabling every state mechanism simultaneously leaves
daily winning by more still. What carries the result is that
$S \propto \ln(1 + UV \cdot E)$ is concave, so at a fixed total budget
(absolute SED, not fractional MED) dividing that budget into more and
smaller sessions always yields more 25(OH)D. Feedback and
photoadaptation modulate the \emph{size} of the gap; neither creates
it.

\textbf{This claim is scoped to budgets of roughly 0.5\,SED/day
absolute dose or more.} The idealised kernel above is concave for
every positive dose, but the published per-session dose-response
evidence bounding how well that idealisation holds does not: four
sources (Bogh 2011, Farrar 2013 FST V, Shih 2018 all-FST, J\"ager
2018) straddle linearity rather than confirming concavity on their own
measured exponents (0.2681--1.2512 on point estimates). A dedicated
test (\texttt{tests/test\_concavity\_assumption.py}) finds the
ordering safe by a wide margin at the roughly-1-SED/day budgets used
for this paper's headline schedules, but \emph{not} protected by the
data at budgets at or below 0.25\,SED/day, where the flip point falls
at or below the single cleanest measured exponent (Shih 2018). One of
the three weekly budgets swept in arm (a) below (1.75\,SED/week,
i.e.\ 0.25\,SED/day averaged over 7 days) sits exactly at that
boundary; the monotonicity finding for that specific cell should be
read with this caveat attached, even though it is not contradicted by
any measurement, only unprotected by one.

The facultative-melanin attenuation coefficient was never a candidate
cause. At its calibrated value the corresponding term is nearly inert
--- the one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis moves steady-state 25(OH)D
by $0.3\,\%$ across its entire prior range (FST V), against
$22.1\,\%$ for the synthesis scalar $A$ --- so the term is inert by
calibration, not merely by ablation. Sweeping it two orders of magnitude past its
literature-implied range never produced a non-daily optimum under a
matched total budget.

The monotonicity result of arm (a) is reported from the melanin-only
rung, and per Section~\ref{sec:ablation} that rung licenses rankings
but not levels. The sign survives at every rung, which is what the
ablation is entitled to establish.

We record one important caveat on the mechanism sub-study. Its
generated findings file was produced under a configuration in which
the facultative-melanin coefficient was pinned at zero, which is no
longer the shipped configuration. Its \emph{magnitudes} are therefore
stale and are not quoted in this paper; its direction and verdict are
unaffected, because the coefficient sweep described above spans the
live value by two orders of magnitude.

\subsection{The vitamin D optimum and the risk optimum coincide}

The two objectives select the same schedules. Under the constrained
problem --- minimise combined skin-cancer risk subject to holding
sufficiency without burning and within a practical session length ---
all six Fitzpatrick types land on the shortest interval available on
the grid, one day (Table~\ref{tab:riskaware}).

This convergence is not a solver artefact, and the reason it is not is
worth stating precisely, because the two constraints bite from
opposite directions. The sufficiency floor eliminates long intervals,
which cannot reach target within the erythema and session-length caps.
Among the schedules that survive, the intermittency term rises steeply
with per-session dose, so the most frequent schedule is also the
lowest-risk one. The risk cap therefore acts as a feasibility test
rather than as a selector.

The agreement is a property of the \emph{constrained} problem, not a
universal one. Over the unconstrained grid the Pareto frontier still
trades off: at FST II over a one-year horizon, the frontier runs from
\SI{4.00}{\SED} daily (8 days below sufficiency, melanoma index
234.931) down to \SI{0.25}{\SED} every 28 days (365 days below
sufficiency, melanoma index 0.002). Removing the requirement to
actually reach sufficiency restores the trade-off immediately, because
the least-risk schedule in an unconstrained problem is always no
exposure at all.

The joint timing-and-dose optimisation shows the same structure from a
third direction. Freeing the spacing exponent against a uniform
control at matched budget raises cumulative-axis (SCC) risk by a
median of $8.32\,\%$ (22 of 24 operating points feasible; range from
a $12.27\,\%$ increase to a $3.41\,\%$ reduction) while reducing
intermittent-axis (BCC/melanoma) risk by a median of $21.59\,\%$
(range $4.34\,\%$ to $63.54\,\%$ reduction), and the two axes must be
read separately rather than
through their geometric mean. Two cells --- FST V and FST VI at 104
sessions/year --- are infeasible under uniform spacing at the erythema
cap and are reported as results rather than dropped. Neither figure
should be read too finely: the solver's own noise diagnostic puts the
floor of a genuine gain at roughly $10\,\%$, so the smaller
cumulative-axis median sits close to that floor while the larger
intermittent-axis shift clears it comfortably. The session budget is
part of the problem statement rather than an add-on: without one the
optimum is degenerate, since at fixed cumulative dose the
intermittency term is minimised by spreading over as many days as
possible, driving the solution to a daily schedule by construction.

\subsection{``Daily'' is conditional on being able to afford daily}
\label{sec:budget}

The preceding results all hold total \SED{} or weekly \SED{} fixed. A
different and equally practical constraint is a fixed number of
sessions per year at a fixed dose each --- the situation of someone who
will take the same exposure every time and can only get outside so
often. Under that constraint the optimal spacing is \emph{not} always
the shortest one, and the reason is instructive.

Fixing the dose at \SI{1.0}{\SED} per session over a 365-day horizon
and searching the uniform gap, the best gap at FST I is 4 days at 52
sessions per year, 2 days at 104 and at 146, and 1 day at 183 and
above. The same pattern holds at FST III, where the best gap at 52
sessions is 3 days.
Only once the session budget is large enough to cover a substantial
fraction of the horizon does the optimum collapse onto the daily
schedule that the fixed-budget analyses select.

The mechanism is decay, not adaptation. With a fixed number of
sessions delivered at the shortest possible gap, the entire programme
is compressed into the first weeks of the year and the remaining
months are pure elimination; 25(OH)D peaks early and then falls for
the rest of the horizon. Spreading a limited number of sessions across
the horizon trades a lower peak for a higher mean and far fewer days
below sufficiency. At FST I with 52 sessions the best schedule spends
209 days below \SI{50}{\nmol}; at 365 sessions it spends 16.

This qualifies the paper's headline claim in a way that matters for
how it should be used. ``More sessions, each smaller'' is the correct
reading at a fixed \emph{dose budget}. ``Go out every day'' is the
correct reading only when the session budget is sufficient to sustain
daily exposure across the whole period of interest. Below that
threshold, the right advice is to distribute the sessions one can
afford evenly rather than to take them as quickly as possible --- and
the cumulative-dose risk index rises steeply with session count
regardless, from 1.000 at 52 sessions to 27.459 at 365 for FST I,
which is the cost side of that same choice.

\subsection{Feasibility is a result, not a gap}

For darker skin types the model returns infeasibility under a range of
session budgets, and we report those cells as findings rather than
filling them by relaxing a constraint. Over the coarse dose grid at a
one-year horizon, no schedule keeps FST V sufficient: its Pareto
frontier runs from \SI{4.00}{\SED} daily with 22 days below
sufficiency down to \SI{0.25}{\SED} every 28 days with all 365 days
below.

This does not contradict Table~\ref{tab:riskaware}, and the
distinction matters. The frontier above is computed over a coarse
grid of fixed per-session doses; the constrained optimisation searches
per-session dose continuously and finds a feasible FST V schedule at
\SI{0.71}{\SED} daily, holding a steady state of 60.8~\nmol{}. What
the coarse frontier shows is that none of a small set of round-numbered
doses happens to work, not that no dose works. Both statements are
reported because a reader who sees only one of them draws the wrong
conclusion.

The tri-objective frontier likewise prints infeasible cells rather
than dropping them, and their pattern is informative: at the lowest
session budgets every spacing is infeasible for every skin type, and
some budgets are feasible under a warped spacing while infeasible
under uniform spacing at the same budget and the same ceiling. The
sufficiency constraint binds at the ramp-in boundary rather than at
the end of the horizon, so a schedule that reaches target faster can
hold a budget that uniform spacing misses.

\paragraph{FST V and VI are reported as a band, not a point.} Within
FST VI itself, resolution now rests on an extrapolated lower-ITA
anchor for the constitutive-melanin mapping, sourced from a single
digitised box plot with its upper anchor and denominator still
unsourced; the calibration set underlying this mapping still contains
no non-lamp (real-sunlight) FST VI vitamin-D datapoint. Treat every
FST VI absolute number in this section as resting on that
sparsely-evidenced anchor, not as a closed question. Three further
sources of dispersion in this cluster are carried through to the
headline feasibility numbers rather than collapsed to a single value.
(i) $\pm 1$ SD on $M_{\mathrm{baseline}}$, from Narea-Jimenez et
al.'s own constitutive-ITA dispersion mapped through the model's
live ITA $\to$ $M$ fit: this moves FST V's minimum feasible session
budget between 146/yr (lighter) and 183/yr (darker) around a shipped
value of 146/yr, and FST VI's between 183/yr (lighter) and 365/yr
(darker) around a shipped value of 260/yr. (ii) The carried
$SC_{\max}$-by-FST IV--VI range, $[0.24, 0.36]$ --
$0.24$ is the value shipped, corroborated by two independent sources;
$0.36$ is de Boer's own FST IV--VI measurement, uncorroborated in
that skin-type range -- leaves the minimum feasible budget unchanged
for both FST V and VI at this parameter pair (146/yr and 260/yr at
either end), though the required minutes per session still rise
toward the $0.36$ end. (iii) $A$ is kept at its point anchor (Young
et al. 2021's full-body arm) rather than treated as a free per-FST
parameter -- $A_{\mathrm{by\_FST}}$ was rejected on identifiability
grounds, since it is exactly degenerate with $K_{\mathrm{const}}$
within any single skin type -- but the twice-weekly (104/yr)
exclusion for FST V is reported with the $A$ value at which it would
flip attached: $A \geq 5.292417$, against a shipped $A = 4.359561$,
i.e.\ the exclusion holds with roughly 21.4\,\% headroom on $A$.
(All three figures computed directly against the shipped parameter
set at time of writing; regenerate from \texttt{RESULTS.md} before
quoting if the parameters move again.)

\subsection{What the model does not pin down}

Two parameters dominate the response, at both priority skin types.
Across documented prior ranges, the constitutive-pigment coefficient
$K_{\mathrm{const}}$ swings steady-state 25(OH)D by 8.98~\nmol{}
(25.8\,\%) at FST V and 8.38~\nmol{} (27.6\,\%) at FST VI, and the
synthesis scalar $A$ by 7.68~\nmol{} (22.1\,\%) at FST V and
5.51~\nmol{} (18.1\,\%) at FST VI. Third is the decay damage term
$k_{\mathrm{damage}}$, at 14.6\,\% / 12.0\,\%, ahead of the stratum
corneum attenuation coefficient $K_{\mathrm{SC}}$ at 5.5\,\% / 4.4\,\%;
every remaining parameter (the facultative-melanin attenuation
$K_{\mathrm{ATTEN}}$, the stratum corneum growth rate
$\alpha_{\mathrm{SC}}$, the tanning decay rate $\beta$, and the two
structurally inert ceilings $\mathrm{SC}_{\max}$ and $D_{\max}$)
contributes at most 1.8\,\%. Parameters at the bottom of that table
cannot be blamed for prediction error however uncertain their citation
trail.

Four parameter pairs are jointly unidentifiable from the current data
(at FST V), in the sense that more than 99\,\% of the steady-state-D
response surface is explained by a single linear combination:
$A \sim D_{\max}$ (ridge 1.0000), $\alpha_{\mathrm{SC}} \sim
\mathrm{SC}_{\max}$ (1.0000), $K_{\mathrm{const}} \sim
K_{\mathrm{ATTEN}}$ (0.9999), and $K_{\mathrm{ATTEN}} \sim
K_{\mathrm{SC}}$ (0.9967). Only their combinations are constrained.
Reporting either member of such a pair as a separately calibrated
value overstates what the data support: melanin and stratum corneum
are both exponential attenuations of a saturating state driven by the
same UV input, so they trade off along a ridge. A caveat attaches to
two of the four: the same diagnostic that generates these ridge
fractions also flags $D_{\max}$ and $\mathrm{SC}_{\max}$ as carrying
zero one-at-a-time sensitivity in their own right, so the
$A \sim D_{\max}$ and $\alpha_{\mathrm{SC}} \sim \mathrm{SC}_{\max}$
ridges may be, at least in part, a mechanical consequence of that
structural inertness rather than confirmed evidence of a data-driven
trade-off; $K_{\mathrm{const}} \sim K_{\mathrm{ATTEN}}$ and
$K_{\mathrm{ATTEN}} \sim K_{\mathrm{SC}}$ are the two pairs this
diagnostic does not flag that way.

Propagating the prior ranges through 400 Monte Carlo draws gives a
median steady-state 25(OH)D of 38.431~\nmol{} (95\,\% range
$[31.668, 49.169]$) at FST V and 32.411~\nmol{} (95\,\% range
$[27.102, 41.726]$) at FST VI. The best interval under a per-\SED{}
gain criterion is no longer as diffuse across draws as it once was,
though it is still not unanimous: at FST V, 1 day is selected in
66.5\,\% of draws, 2 days in 20.8\,\%, 3 days in 11.2\,\%, 4 days in
1.2\,\% and 5 days in 0.2\,\%; at FST VI the concentration is sharper
still, 1 day in 89.8\,\%, 2 days in 9.8\,\% and 3 days in 0.5\,\%. The
plurality has moved from a spread of nearby intervals toward the
shortest one, but roughly a third of the FST V draws still select 2 or
3 days, so a single headline number would still imply more precision
than the evidence supports.

\subsection{The one held-out test now fails}

Nothing in the model is calibrated to the Webb and Kift dataset
\citep{webbkift2018}, which makes it the only genuinely out-of-sample
evidence available. It reports that FST V requires 2.5--3.0 times the
dose of white Caucasians for an equivalent 25(OH)D response. The model
returns $4.12\times$, above the band: a \textbf{fail}, on the only
comparison in this work that was not fitted to the data it is checked
against. This is a change from the previous calibration, in which the
model returned $2.87\times$ and passed near the upper edge of the
band; the same comparison now fails by a comparable margin on the
other side.

A diagnostic minutes ratio computed under the same conditions gives
$5.30$ ($68.2$ vs $361.6$ minutes) against a reported $2.78$. That
figure is not a second, independent check but an algebraic
restatement of the same underlying measurement, and is gated as
diagnostic context rather than as an independent check.

The model now over-predicts the skin-type penalty on this benchmark,
where the previous calibration under-predicted it only slightly. The
relevant literature disagreement is not, as an earlier version of this
work recorded, between lamp-based and naturalistic exposure. The
source of the 2.5--3.0 band is itself a matched-dose, simulated-sunlight
trial at approximately 35\,\% BSA against a matched-dose white
comparator \citep{farrar2013}, and the study to which the
constitutive-pigment coefficient is calibrated reports a ratio of
1.3--1.4 under equally matched-dose lamp conditions \citep{young2020}.
Those two source studies disagree with each other, lamp against lamp,
by a margin this failure does not by itself resolve: the model now
sits outside the range reported by \emph{either} of them. A single
failing comparison against a band drawn from one source study does not
adjudicate that conflict, and should not be read in isolation as an
indictment of the constitutive-pigment coefficient; it should be read,
at minimum, as removing the only piece of genuinely out-of-sample
corroboration this model previously had.

\subsection{Validation status, disaggregated}

The validation suite does not reduce to one pooled pass count, and
reporting it as one is itself a source of drift: an earlier draft of
this paper reported ``14 of 14'', and the pipeline's own report at one
point pooled the same checks as ``13 of 14''. Both figures conflate
two gates that must be kept apart. Required checks pass 10 of 10;
checks demoted to warning-only (for a documented reason, such as
circularity) pass 3 of 4.

Orthogonal to that pass/fail split, and the number that actually
bounds what the suite licenses, is an evidence-tier breakdown across
all 14 checks: \textsc{internal} 3/3, \textsc{directional} 3/3,
\textsc{circular} 4/4, \textsc{quantitative} 2/2, and
\textsc{not\_evidence} 1/2.

Checks tagged \textsc{internal} verify that the simulator computes its
own documented mathematics --- that numerical integration matches the
analytic solution of the same ODE, or that a single-bolus response
equals a value rebuilt from the model's own live parameters. These
cannot fail for biological reasons. Checks tagged \textsc{circular}
compare model output against the dataset from which the relevant
parameters were fitted; the stratum corneum check compares a
trajectory against the data used to fit the parameters that generate
it. Checks tagged \textsc{directional} assert only a sign, with no
numeric target.

Both \textsc{quantitative}-tier checks currently pass: a matched-dose
FST V trial \citep{farrar2013} and a seven-scenario reference-protocol
benchmark spanning de Boer 2025, Farrar 2013, Young 2021 and Felton
2016. (An earlier draft of this paper, on the previous calibration,
reported a \textsc{quantitative} failure on the
\texttt{felton2016\_fst5\_matched} scenario within that benchmark;
under the current parameters that scenario now passes, and the row is
2/2.) The one failing check in the full 14-check suite is instead
tagged \textsc{not\_evidence} and is self-labelled as such in its own
source: a MED-derived ratio (Kallioglu 2024, run through Hollick's
rule) that its own docstring states is ``NOT vitamin-D evidence'' and
must not be counted as such. The pass counts above are real; they are
not evidence of predictive accuracy on the one axis that actually
matters here, the skin-type dose ratio, because neither
\textsc{quantitative} check tests it. The held-out benchmark in the
preceding section is not counted in either the pass/fail split or the
evidence-tier breakdown above, and it is the only genuinely
out-of-sample test in this work \emph{on that axis}; it now fails,
narrowly. Read together rather than selectively, the two facts say the
same thing the rest of this section argues: the model's external
numeric support on the skin-type dose ratio is thin and currently
unfavourable, and neither a passing in-suite count nor a single
failing held-out comparison should be read as settling the question on
its own.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{A trade-off that dissolves under a budget}

The central finding is structural rather than parametric. Once total
UV is fixed and sufficiency is required, the vitamin D objective and
the melanoma/BCC objective stop competing, because they are both
minimised by reducing per-session intensity while holding cumulative
dose. Vitamin D benefits because a concave dose-response wastes less
of a small session; melanoma and BCC risk falls because the pathway
that carries that risk is intermittency, not accumulation
\citep{elwood1997,leiter2009}. The squamous-cell axis does track
cumulative dose and therefore does not improve, which is why we report
the two axes separately and treat their geometric mean as a ranking
convenience only.

This has a practical reading that is worth stating in plain terms:
under the reference conditions modelled here, the advice ``go outside
briefly and often'' is not a compromise between two competing goods.
It is the joint optimum of both.

\subsection{Two questions hiding inside one word}

The arm (a) and arm (b) results answer different questions and give
opposite-looking answers, which is precisely why they must be
separated. Compressing a weekly budget into fewer days is harmful.
Weighting a longer programme toward its beginning is neutral on where
you end up and strongly beneficial on how fast you get there.

The methodological point generalises beyond this model. The sign of
the taper effect depends on whether the outcome is terminal or
horizon-integrated, and that dependence \emph{widens} with the length
of the horizon. Any schedule-shape claim that does not name its
outcome measure and confirm that session count was matched is
under-specified. Our own back-loading artefact is the cautionary case:
a schedule that leaves a subject deficient for most of a year wins the
terminal measure by 22.3\,\% purely by exploiting the averaging
window.

\subsection{Where the interval ranking is safe and the minutes are not}

The interval ranking depends on the \emph{shape} of the dose-response
curve rather than its scale, which is why it survives the model's
considerable parametric uncertainty: the two parameters that dominate
steady-state 25(OH)D, $K_{\mathrm{const}}$ and $A$, are both
multiplicative scalars in Equation~\eqref{eq:synth} and do not alter
the concavity that produces the ordering.

Absolute durations have no such protection. They inherit the full
uncertainty of the synthesis scalar, the pigment coefficient, the
geometry factor and the exposed-area assumption, and they additionally
assume the ambient UV index holds constant for the whole session. The
geometry factor alone is a scalar prior carrying at least $\pm40\,\%$
uncertainty, and it multiplies every duration reported here.

We therefore draw a sharp line: the interval recommendation is the
usable output of this work, and no absolute-minutes figure in it
should be treated as a dosing instruction.

\subsection{Implications for how guidance is expressed}

Sun-exposure guidance is conventionally issued as a duration paired
with a frequency, and the two are treated as independently
adjustable. The results here suggest that this is the wrong
factorisation. What determines the plateau is the product of frequency
and per-session dose --- the budget --- together with how finely that
budget is divided; what determines the speed of approach is the
weighting of the budget over time; and what determines melanoma and
BCC risk is per-session intensity, which is the budget divided by the
frequency. Guidance expressed as a single duration therefore
under-determines all three quantities at once.

A more defensible formulation states a budget and a minimum
frequency, with the duration derived. This also makes the safety
constraint explicit rather than implicit: the schedules that fail in
our grid fail by requiring a session above the erythemal threshold,
and that failure is invisible in advice phrased purely as a weekly
duration.

The qualification in Section~\ref{sec:budget} should travel with any
such recommendation. The daily optimum is conditional on a session
budget sufficient to sustain it; for anyone who cannot get outside
that often, the correct advice inverts from ``as often as possible''
to ``as evenly as possible''. Guidance that states only the former
gives the wrong instruction to precisely the population that finds
adherence hardest.

\subsection{Relation to the photoadaptation hypothesis}

The hypothesis that motivated this analysis --- that photoadaptation
should reward spacing exposures apart --- is not supported, and the
reason is quantitative rather than conceptual. Photoadaptation is real
in the model and is directionally validated against an external
protocol \citep{deboer2025}: melanin rises, stratum corneum thickens,
and synthesis falls. But at the sub-erythemal doses that any sensible
schedule uses, the melanin state barely moves over a twelve-week
protocol, and the attenuation it produces is an order of magnitude
smaller than the concavity effect it would need to overcome.

This is a case where the correct answer was available analytically and
the modelling effort served mainly to establish that no parameter
value in the plausible range overturns it.

\subsection{What would resolve the unidentifiability}

The four unidentifiable pairs are not equally tractable, and
distinguishing them indicates where measurement effort would actually
pay. $K_{\mathrm{const}} \sim K_{\mathrm{ATTEN}}$ and
$K_{\mathrm{ATTEN}} \sim K_{\mathrm{SC}}$ --- the constitutive and
facultative attenuation coefficients against each other --- are
unidentifiable because the available calibration data are group
medians at a small number of timepoints. Individual-level longitudinal
trajectories of stratum corneum thickness and melanin index under a
controlled protocol would break both ridges directly, because the
ridge exists only when the rate and the asymptote cannot be observed
separately, and a within-subject time course observes them separately
by construction. The other two pairs, $A \sim D_{\max}$ and
$\alpha_{\mathrm{SC}} \sim \mathrm{SC}_{\max}$, are a different case:
the same sensitivity analysis that generates the ridge fractions shows
$D_{\max}$ and $\mathrm{SC}_{\max}$ each carrying zero one-at-a-time
sensitivity on the current parameter set, so those two ridges may be,
in part, a mechanical artefact of that structural inertness rather
than a data-limited trade-off between a growth rate and its ceiling,
and more timepoints would not obviously resolve them.

The pair joining the synthesis scalar to the feedback saturation
constant is different in kind. Those two parameters trade off because
the calibration data span a narrow range of baseline 25(OH)D; the
feedback term is only weakly excited when every subject starts near
the same concentration. Resolving it requires calibration data across
a wide baseline range rather than more timepoints at one baseline. The
same limitation explains why the feedback form itself is weakly
constrained: the inflection is inherited from a breakpoint fitted on a
different axis \citep{scholze2026} rather than estimated from the
25(OH)D response, and the exponent cannot be fitted from that source
at all.

We report these as ridges rather than as calibrated values because the
alternative --- quoting a point estimate for each member --- would
represent as independently determined a quantity that the data
constrain only in combination.

\section{Limitations}

\paragraph{Uncalibrated priors.} The stratum corneum parameters
($\mathrm{SC}_{\max}$, $\alpha_{\mathrm{SC}}$, $K_{\mathrm{SC}}$) are
priors rather than calibrated values, and the stratum corneum decay
rate is an interior, uncalibrated choice: no source measures
regression of stratum corneum hyperplasia directly. Any absolute
25(OH)D prediction should be read with that flagged.

\paragraph{Light-skin extrapolation.} The synthesis scalar $A$ is
anchored on a study whose subjects were FST I--II only, and whose
authors explicitly disclaim generalisation beyond those types
\citep{young2021pnas}. Because $A$ is a whole-model scalar applied at
every skin type, every absolute prediction for FST V--VI is a
light-skin extrapolation. This is mitigated but not removed by the
fact that the pigment gradient is anchored separately on matched-dose
data in darker skin \citep{young2020,farrar2013,felton2016,kift2013}:
what is extrapolated is the overall level, not the gradient. Given
that darker skin is the population for which sun-exposure guidance is
least adequate, this is the limitation we regard as most consequential.

\paragraph{Unidentifiability.} Four parameter pairs are jointly
unidentifiable, though two of the four may be partly a mechanical
artefact of near-zero standalone sensitivity in $D_{\max}$ and
$\mathrm{SC}_{\max}$ rather than confirmed data-driven ridges (see
``What the model does not pin down'' above). Individual-level
longitudinal stratum corneum and melanin trajectories would break the
two genuinely data-limited ridges ($K_{\mathrm{const}} \sim
K_{\mathrm{ATTEN}}$ and $K_{\mathrm{ATTEN}} \sim K_{\mathrm{SC}}$);
group medians cannot.

\paragraph{Fixed scenario.} No location, date, weather, posture or
behaviour is modelled. Durations are conditional on the frozen
reference scenario throughout.

\paragraph{Dietary and supplemental intake.} The model has no oral
intake term. Real populations obtain a non-trivial baseline from diet
and supplementation, so the absolute sufficiency conclusions
--- particularly the infeasibility results for darker skin --- describe
a sun-only counterfactual rather than a realistic total-intake
scenario.

\paragraph{Risk index scope.} Cross-skin-type comparison of risk index
\emph{values} depends on a susceptibility axis derived from data
covering only extreme phototypes. Interval and minutes columns do not
depend on it.

\paragraph{Single external test.} We have one genuinely held-out
benchmark, and it fails --- narrowly, running just above the reported
band, and on a single comparison against a band drawn from one source
study. A model with one out-of-sample test has weak external support
regardless of how many internal checks pass, and this single failing
comparison should not be read as settling the question either, given
that the band itself is drawn from a source study that disagrees with
the model's own calibration anchor by a comparable margin.

\section{Conclusions}

For a fixed UV budget (absolute SED, not fractional MED) under a
frozen reference exposure scenario, dividing exposure into more and
smaller sessions raises steady-state 25(OH)D monotonically, by
9.53--28.59\,\% across the tested grid, and the mechanism is the
concavity of the logarithmic dose-response rather than photoadaptation
or product-feedback inhibition, both of which modulate the magnitude
of the effect without changing its sign. This claim is well protected
by the available per-session curvature evidence at budgets of roughly
0.5\,SED/day or more, which covers most of the tested grid; at the
lowest tested budget (0.25\,SED/day) the evidence straddles linearity
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}) and neither confirms nor rules out the
same ordering.

Redistributing a fixed horizon budget from flat to front-loaded does
not raise the plateau at any horizon tested, but reduces accumulated
deficiency by 47.1\,\% at 84 days and 63.8\,\% at 365 days, and
reaches sufficiency in days rather than months. The two claims that
``front-loading'' can denote must therefore be stated separately, with
the outcome measure named, because their signs differ and the
divergence widens with horizon.

Under a constrained optimisation requiring sufficiency without
erythema, all six Fitzpatrick types select the shortest available
interval, so the vitamin D objective and the melanoma/BCC objective
agree rather than trade off. That agreement is a property of the
constrained problem; over an unconstrained grid the frontier still
trades off, because the least-risk unconstrained schedule is no
exposure.

These conclusions are ordinal and should be used as such. The model's
one held-out external test currently fails, running just above its
reported band, four of its parameter pairs are jointly unidentifiable,
and its synthesis scalar is calibrated on light-skinned subjects only.
The interval ranking survives that uncertainty because it depends on
curve shape rather than curve scale; the absolute skin-type dose ratio
does not, and the held-out failure is a direct warning against
treating it as calibrated. No absolute duration reported here is a
dosing instruction.
